🧮 Essential Calculations for This Mock

Calculating Mr (Relative Formula Mass):

Find the Mr of Ca(NO₃)₂

  1. Ca = 40
  2. N = 14 (× 2 = 28)
  3. O = 16 (× 6 = 96)
  4. Total Mr = 40 + 28 + 96 = 164

Calculating Ar from Isotopes (HT):

Chlorine has two isotopes: Cl-35 (75%) and Cl-37 (25%). Calculate the Ar.

  1. Ar = (mass × abundance) for each isotope, then divide by 100
  2. Ar = (35 × 75) + (37 × 25) ÷ 100
  3. Ar = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100
  4. Ar = 3550 ÷ 100 = 35.5

Remember: Always use values from periodic table, show working clearly!

🎯 Essential Higher Tier Calculations

  • Moles: n = m/Mr (moles = mass ÷ relative formula mass)
  • Avogadro's constant: 6.02 × 10²³ particles per mole
  • Concentration (mol/dm³): c = n/V (concentration = moles ÷ volume)
  • Converting units: 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³
  • Gas volumes: 1 mole of any gas = 24 dm³ at RTP
  • Percentage yield: (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
  • Atom economy: (Mr of desired product ÷ sum of Mr of all products) × 100
  • Titration calculations: Using n₁V₁/a = n₂V₂/b
  • Limiting reactants: Identifying which reactant runs out first

📐 Key Equations You MUST Memorise

n = m/Mr
c = n/V (where V is in dm³)
% yield = (actual/theoretical) × 100
% atom economy = (Mr desired product/Mr all products) × 100
For gases: V = n × 24 dm³ (at RTP)

📚 Mark Scheme Language for Calculations

  • Relative atomic mass: "Weighted average mass of atoms of an element compared to 1/12th mass of carbon-12"
  • The mole: "Amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles"
  • Limiting reactant: "The reactant that is completely used up first, determining the amount of product formed"
  • Why percentage yield less than 100%: "Incomplete reaction, side reactions, product lost during separation/purification"
  • Importance of high atom economy: "Less waste, more sustainable, more economically viable"
  • Titration endpoint: "Point where indicator changes colour permanently"

⚠️ Common Higher Tier Calculation Errors

  • Units: Always convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000
  • Mole ratios: Use balanced equation coefficients - if 2A → 3B, then 2 moles A gives 3 moles B
  • Significant figures: Match your answer to the least precise value in the question
  • Atom economy: Use Mr of ALL products (including waste), not just reactants
  • Titration: Average only concordant results (within 0.1 cm³)
  • Show ALL working: Even if calculator gives answer directly

📝 Calculation Strategy

  1. Step 1: Write down what you know (with units)
  2. Step 2: Write down what you need to find
  3. Step 3: Choose the correct equation
  4. Step 4: Rearrange if necessary
  5. Step 5: Substitute values (check units match)
  6. Step 6: Calculate and include units in answer
  7. Step 7: Check answer is sensible

❓ Higher Tier Calculation FAQs

Q: How do I know when to use moles in a calculation?
A: If the question involves reacting masses, concentrations, or gas volumes, you'll need moles as the connecting concept.
Q: What's the difference between percentage yield and atom economy?
A: Yield compares actual vs theoretical amount of desired product. Atom economy compares desired product vs ALL products (shows efficiency of reaction design).
Q: In titrations, why do we repeat until we get concordant results?
A: To improve reliability and reduce random errors. Only concordant results (within 0.1 cm³) should be averaged.

📚 Mark Scheme Language - Use These Exact Phrases!

  • Atom: "The smallest part of an element that can exist"
  • Element: "A substance containing only one type of atom"
  • Compound: "Two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions"
  • Mixture: "Two or more elements or compounds not chemically combined"
  • Isotopes: "Atoms of the same element with same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons"
  • Dalton's model: "Atoms are solid, indivisible spheres; each element has identical atoms"
  • Thomson's plum pudding: "Ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded throughout"
  • Rutherford's nuclear model: "Mass concentrated in small positive nucleus, electrons orbit in empty space"
  • Bohr model: "Electrons orbit nucleus in fixed energy levels/shells"
  • Rutherford's conclusions: "Most alpha particles passed straight through (atom mostly empty space), some deflected (positive charge concentrated in nucleus), few bounced back (nucleus very small but dense)"
  • Neutron discovery: "James Chadwick provided evidence that neutral particles (neutrons) exist in nucleus"
  • Mendeleev's periodic table: "Arranged by atomic mass but left gaps for undiscovered elements and swapped some elements to fit properties"
  • Modern periodic table: "Arranged by atomic number (number of protons)"
  • Filtration: "Separates insoluble solid from liquid"
  • Crystallisation: "Separates soluble solid from solution by evaporating solvent"
  • Distillation: "Separates liquids with different boiling points"
  • Chromatography: "Separates mixtures based on different solubilities in mobile/stationary phase"
  • Calculating Mr/RFM: "Add up all the relative atomic masses of atoms in the formula"
  • Calculating Ar from isotopes (HT): "Ar = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100"
  • Group 1 reactivity: "Reactivity increases down the group because outer electron is further from nucleus, so less attraction, easier to lose"
  • Group 7 reactivity: "Reactivity decreases down the group because outer shell is further from nucleus, so less attraction for electrons, harder to gain"
  • Calculating Ar from isotopes (HT): "Ar = Σ(isotope mass × % abundance) ÷ 100"
  • Transition metals vs Group 1: "Transition metals: harder, stronger, higher melting points, less reactive, form colored compounds, can have different charges"

⚠️ Top Tips from Examiner Reports

  • Many students confuse "atom" with "element" - an element contains many atoms!
  • Common error with atomic models: Don't say Rutherford "discovered" the nucleus - he provided evidence for it
  • Alpha scattering: Most went straight through (NOT all) - this shows atom is mostly empty space
  • When describing Group 1 reactions, be specific: say "bubbles" not "gas produced"
  • Electronic structures: Remember maximum electrons = 2,8,8 for GCSE
  • Common error: Saying elements in same group have same number of electrons (they have same number of OUTER electrons)
  • Always specify "outer shell" or "outermost shell" when discussing reactivity

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I calculate relative atomic mass from isotope data? (HT)
A: Multiply each isotope mass by its % abundance, add them all up, then divide by 100. E.g., Cl-35 (75%) and Cl-37 (25%): Ar = (35×75 + 37×25)/100 = 35.5
Q: Why was Mendeleev's periodic table accepted even though some elements were in the wrong order by mass?
A: His predictions about undiscovered elements were proved correct when they were found. Elements fitted better by properties than strict mass order.
Q: Why are noble gases unreactive?
A: They have a full outer shell of electrons, so they don't need to gain, lose or share electrons.
Q: How do I remember which way reactivity goes in Groups 1 and 7?
A: Group 1 LOSES electrons (easier when further from nucleus) - reactivity increases down. Group 7 GAINS electrons (harder when further from nucleus) - reactivity decreases down.
Q: Why did the atomic model change over time?
A: New experimental evidence led to changes. Thomson discovered electrons (led to plum pudding). Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed nucleus exists (led to nuclear model). Further experiments showed electrons in shells (Bohr model).
Q: What's the difference between mass number and relative atomic mass?
A: Mass number = protons + neutrons (whole number for one atom). Relative atomic mass = average mass considering all isotopes (often decimal).

▼

🎯 Most Important Content (High Exam Frequency)

  • Covalent bonding: Non-metal + non-metal, electron sharing
  • Properties comparison: Ionic vs simple molecular vs giant covalent
  • Diamond vs Graphite vs Graphene: Structure and properties explanation
  • Fullerenes and nanotubes (HT): Structure, properties and uses

  • Ionic formulae: Working out formulae using charges

📚 Mark Scheme Language - Use These Exact Phrases!

  • Ionic bonding: "Electrons transferred from metal atoms to non-metal atoms forming oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic forces"
  • Covalent bonding: "Atoms share pairs of electrons"
  • Why ionic compounds conduct when molten/dissolved: "Ions are free to move and carry charge"
  • Why ionic compounds don't conduct when solid: "Ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice"
  • Why simple molecular substances have low melting points: "Weak intermolecular forces between molecules require little energy to overcome"
  • Why diamond is hard: "Each carbon atom forms 4 strong covalent bonds in a giant 3D structure"
  • Why graphite conducts: "Each carbon forms 3 bonds, leaving 1 delocalised electron per carbon that can move and carry charge"
  • Why graphene is strong: "Single layer of carbon atoms, each forming 3 strong covalent bonds in hexagonal structure"

⚠️ Top Tips from Examiner Reports

  • Students often confuse intermolecular forces with covalent bonds - be clear about the difference!
  • When explaining properties, always link structure → property with "because" or "so"
  • Dot-cross diagrams: Only show outer shell electrons, use dots for one element, crosses for other
  • Common mistake: Saying covalent bonds break when simple molecular substances melt (they don't!)
  • Remember: High melting point = strong forces BETWEEN particles need breaking

📝 Revision Strategies

  • Comparison tables: Create a table comparing ionic, simple molecular, giant covalent properties
  • Flashcards: Ion charges for common elements (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻ etc.)
  • Practice drawing: Dot-cross diagrams for NaCl, MgO, H₂O, CO₂, NH₃
  • Memory aid: "METALS LOSE to become POSITIVE" (form cations)
  • 3D models: Use molecular model kits or online simulations to visualize structures

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I know if a substance is ionic or covalent?
A: Look at the elements - metal + non-metal = ionic, non-metal + non-metal = covalent
Q: Why doesn't water conduct electricity if it contains ions (H⁺ and OH⁻)?
A: Pure water has very few ions. It only conducts well when ionic compounds dissolve in it.
Q: What's the difference between graphite and graphene?
A: Graphite has many layers of carbon atoms, graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms.

Topic 3: Rates of Reaction and Equilibria

▼

🎯 Most Important Content (High Exam Frequency)

  • Collision theory: Particles must collide with sufficient energy (activation energy)
  • Factors affecting rate: Temperature, concentration, surface area, catalysts
  • Required practical: Effect of concentration on rate (sodium thiosulfate or Mg + HCl)
  • Measuring rate: Amount of reactant used or product formed ÷ time
  • Rate graphs: Gradient = rate (steeper = faster)
  • Reversible reactions: ⇌ symbol, examples like ammonium chloride
  • Le Chatelier's principle (HT): How equilibrium shifts with changes
  • Effect of conditions on equilibrium (HT): Temperature, pressure, concentration

📚 Mark Scheme Language - Use These Exact Phrases!

  • Why temperature increases rate: "Particles move faster, more frequent collisions, more particles have energy ≥ activation energy"
  • Why concentration increases rate: "More particles in same volume, more frequent collisions"
  • Why surface area increases rate: "More particles exposed, more frequent collisions"
  • Catalyst definition: "Increases rate of reaction without being used up by providing alternative pathway with lower activation energy"
  • Equilibrium: "Rate of forward reaction equals rate of backward reaction"
  • Le Chatelier for temperature (HT): "If temperature increases, equilibrium shifts in endothermic direction to oppose the change"
  • Le Chatelier for pressure (HT): "If pressure increases, equilibrium shifts to side with fewer gas molecules"
  • Le Chatelier for concentration (HT): "If concentration of reactant increases, equilibrium shifts to right to use up added reactant"

⚠️ Top Tips from Examiner Reports

  • Always mention BOTH frequency of collisions AND energy when explaining temperature effects
  • Rate graphs: Remember rate is shown by gradient - the steeper the line, the faster the reaction
  • Common error: Saying catalyst "speeds up" particles (it doesn't - it lowers activation energy)
  • Practical write-ups must include: Independent, dependent and control variables

📝 Revision Strategies

  • Graph practice: Draw and interpret rate graphs - identify fastest rate (steepest gradient)
  • Flashcards: Factors affecting rate with full explanations using collision theory
  • Understanding rate: How to measure rate and what affects it
  • Method writing: Practice writing full methods for rate practicals
  • Visual memory: Draw particle diagrams showing effect of each factor

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I know which way equilibrium shifts? (HT)
A: System opposes the change - if you increase temperature, it shifts to decrease it (endothermic direction). If pressure increases, shifts to side with fewer gas molecules. If concentration increases, shifts to use up the added substance.
Q: What's the difference between rate and time?
A: Rate is speed of reaction (high = fast). Time is duration (high = slow). They're inversely related.
Q: Do catalysts appear in the equation?
A: No, they're written above the arrow because they're not used up.

Topic 4: Energy Changes (excluding metals/extraction)

▼

🎯 Most Important Content (High Exam Frequency)

  • Exothermic reactions: Release energy to surroundings (temperature rises)
  • Endothermic reactions: Take in energy from surroundings (temperature falls)
  • Required practical: Temperature changes - neutralisation and displacement
  • Reaction profiles: Drawing and interpreting energy diagrams
  • Bond breaking and making (HT): Breaking bonds requires energy, making bonds releases energy
  • Activation energy: Minimum energy for reaction to occur
  • Overall energy change: Difference between reactants and products

📚 Mark Scheme Language - Use These Exact Phrases!

  • Exothermic: "Energy transferred from reaction to surroundings"
  • Endothermic: "Energy transferred from surroundings to reaction"
  • Examples of exothermic: "Combustion, neutralisation, oxidation"
  • Examples of endothermic: "Thermal decomposition, citric acid + sodium hydrogen carbonate"
  • Breaking bonds: "Requires energy input (endothermic process)"
  • Making bonds: "Releases energy (exothermic process)"

⚠️ Top Tips from Examiner Reports

  • Common confusion: Breaking bonds REQUIRES energy (endothermic process)
  • Making bonds RELEASES energy (exothermic process)
  • Reaction profiles: Products below reactants = exothermic, products above = endothermic
  • Temperature practical: Use polystyrene cup for insulation, lid to reduce heat loss
  • Remember: Temperature change shows energy transfer direction

📝 Revision Strategies

  • Memory aid: "EXothermic = EXit" (energy exits to surroundings)
  • Practice drawing: Reaction profiles for both endo and exothermic reactions
  • Understanding bonds: Breaking bonds needs energy, making bonds releases energy
  • Practical revision: Write out full method for temperature change practical
  • Real-life examples: Hand warmers (exo), sports injury packs (endo)

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Why is breaking bonds endothermic?
A: Energy is needed to overcome the attractive forces holding atoms together.
Q: How do I remember which energy change is which?
A: Exothermic = temperature goes up (releases heat). Endothermic = temperature goes down (absorbs heat).
Q: What happens to energy in chemical reactions?
A: Energy is conserved - it's transferred between the chemical store and thermal energy of surroundings.

📚 Mark Scheme Language - Use These Exact Phrases!

  • Potable water: "Water that is safe to drink, contains low levels of dissolved salts and microbes"
  • Pure water (chemistry): "Contains only H₂O molecules, no dissolved substances"
  • Finite resource: "Being used up faster than it can be replaced"
  • Sustainable development: "Meeting current needs without compromising future generations"
  • Why recycle: "Conserves limited resources, reduces energy use, reduces landfill, reduces pollution"
  • LCA limitation: "Some aspects involve value judgements/opinions not measurable data"

⚠️ Top Tips from Examiner Reports

  • Don't confuse potable with pure water - potable water is NOT chemically pure!
  • Desalination: Remember it requires lots of energy (expensive)
  • LCA questions: Consider ALL four stages, not just manufacture
  • Practical: Distilled water should have NO dissolved solids (test with evaporation)
  • Be specific about environmental impacts: "reduces CO₂ emissions" not just "better for environment"

📝 Revision Strategies

  • Flow diagrams: Draw water treatment and sewage treatment processes
  • LCA practice: Compare plastic vs paper bags considering all 4 stages
  • Flashcards: Key definitions - potable, finite, renewable, sustainable
  • Real examples: List finite (oil, metals) and renewable (wood, cotton) resources
  • Practical method: Write out distillation method step-by-step with diagram

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What's the difference between potable and pure water?
A: Potable = safe to drink but contains some dissolved substances. Pure = only H₂O molecules.
Q: Why is recycling not always the best option?
A: It can use energy, create pollution from transport, and some materials degrade when recycled.
Q: How do I answer LCA questions?
A: Consider extraction → manufacture → use → disposal for BOTH products being compared.
